THE VILLAGE OF MORTENWALL HAS BEEN WAKING EAST FOR NINETY-THREE CONSECUTIVE DAYS

The geologist sent to investigate was found on day ninety — facing east, smiling in a manner none of her colleagues had ever witnessed before.
By Elspeth Ravenwood · Mortenwall Correspondent · Virentis XIV

Residents of Mortenwall — a village of four hundred souls on the grey edge of Sudenhollow Moor — have for ninety-three mornings awakened in their beds facing due east, regardless of how they fell asleep. The phenomenon was first noted by Greta Haas, sixty-two, an insomniac who is certain she fell asleep facing south and woke with her nose pressed to the east-facing wall. "I thought I had walked in my sleep," she told this correspondent. "Then my husband said the same thing had happened to him. Then my neighbor said it. By Thursday we had stopped talking about it and just started facing east."

By the fortieth day, the village's church steeple — which has faced north for three hundred years — had rotated. Its weathervane now points east regardless of wind direction. By day sixty-seven, the river Holden, which has run westward through the valley since cartographers first recorded it, had quietly changed its course.

"She was smiling," said the assistant who found Dr. Brennan in the field. "Not a smile I had seen on her face before. Not a smile that had a reason I could name. Just east, and smiling, as if she had been told something she had always half expected."

Dr. Maren Brennan, thirty-nine, geologist with the National Survey, arrived on day eighty-seven. Her preliminary report was submitted on day eighty-nine. She was found at dawn on day ninety-one standing in the field east of the church. She was facing east. She has not spoken of what she saw in the night between those days. She says only that it was "perfectly clear." The village has not requested a replacement investigator. When asked why, the mayor said: "We haven't been asked to stop."

Horror  ·  Obituaries Desk

WOMAN RECEIVES HER OWN OBITUARY — IT LISTS A MIDDLE NAME SHE HAS TOLD TO NO LIVING PERSON

The newspaper has no record of printing it. An addendum arrived the following morning with a correction to her daughter's name.
By Staff Correspondent · City Bureau

Agnes Marlowe, sixty-seven, of the Colwick district, found a full-column obituary for herself in Tuesday's edition of this newspaper. Her cause of death: unspecified. Her favourite song: correct — a recording she has not played in forty years, had mentioned to no one, and had half-forgotten until she saw it printed there. Her middle name — which she has shared with nobody since her mother died in 1991 — was spelled without error. The funeral, the obituary said, would be "intimate, attended by the people who mattered."

Mrs. Marlowe called our offices at eight-fifteen that morning. Our circulation staff found no record of the submission. Our typesetter found no record of the layout. The archive copy of Tuesday's paper does not contain the column.

The following morning, a slip of paper was found beneath her door in the typeface of our standard corrections column. It corrected the spelling of her daughter's name. Mrs. Marlowe does not know when it was delivered, nor by whom, nor how it came to know there had been a spelling error she had not reported.

Horror  ·  Domestic

MAN'S HANDWRITING TRANSFORMS OVERNIGHT INTO HIS DEAD FATHER'S — THE LIST ENDS: COME FIND ME, WRITTEN THREE TIMES, SMALLER EACH TIME

By Harlan Foss · Domestic Affairs

Theodore Wren, fifty-one, of Ashburn Lane, wrote a grocery list Monday evening in his own hand: milk, bread, candles, matches. He woke Tuesday to find it rewritten in handwriting he recognized immediately and without question as his father's — dead seventeen years. The slightly rightward slant was identical to letters his father had written to him during school. The unclosed lowercase e, which his father had never corrected, was there as well.

Mr. Wren rewrote the list. He did not sleep well. Wednesday morning, the list had been altered again. The original four items remained, in his father's hand. At the bottom, in progressively smaller script — as if the author were becoming aware of diminishing space, or diminishing time — were three additions:

come find me
come find me
come find me

Mr. Wren has purchased the grocery items. He has not acted on the additions. He reports that his own handwriting has, in the days since, begun to tilt rightward. He has not told his family. He says this is because there is not, as yet, anything to tell.

Horror  ·  Festival Report

HARROW'S EIGHTH ANNUAL FOG FESTIVAL WELCOMES TWELVE NEW STRANGERS — NONE OF THE ORIGINAL SEVEN HAVE AGED SINCE 1948

By Constance Vale · Harrow Bureau

The town of Harrow, population eight hundred, has since October of 1948 welcomed seven strangers from the autumn fog — one or two per decade, arriving from the direction of the moor, speaking in perfect local dialect, knowing the residents' names without introduction, and unable to account for where they came from. The welcome committee long ago stopped pressing the question.

What the committee has noted — and what the town physician has now certified in a document filed with the county — is that none of the seven have aged since their arrival. The first, who gave his name as Ander, appeared then to be approximately thirty years old. He appears thirty today. He works at the mill. He is well-liked. He does not speak of the fog except to say he remembers it being warm.

This October, the fog arrived eleven days early. Twelve strangers walked out of it. The twelfth is, by all accounts, not yet ready to be indoors. Each evening they stand at the eastern edge of the village and face the moor. Ander has begun doing the same.

Horror  ·  Science

CONFIRMED: THE SOUND AT THE OCEAN'S DEEPEST POINT IS LAUGHTER — REPEATING EVERY ELEVEN MINUTES, UNDISTORTED BY PRESSURE

By Dr. Iris Moll · Science Correspondent

The hydrophone deployed at thirty-six thousand feet in the Vantherian Trench captured what NOAA initially classified as geological resonance. The interval is precise: eleven minutes, zero seconds. The duration is consistent: four-point-seven seconds per occurrence. Upon spectral analysis, the acoustic team confirmed — and then confirmed again, using a second lab — that the sound is unmistakably human laughter.

It is not echoing. It is not distorted by the eleven thousand pounds per square inch of pressure at that depth. It does not attenuate with distance. The laughter is: "the kind that follows something the laughing party already knew."

"It is not frightened laughter," the report states in its final paragraph. "That is the part that none of us can account for, and that all of us find, in different ways, the most difficult to set aside at the end of the working day."

The hydrophone has been left in place. The laughter has not stopped. The interval has not varied by a single second in fourteen months. No organism capable of producing such a sound is known to exist at that depth. The recording has been played, at time of printing, forty-one million times.

CLOCKMAKER SLOWED TIME ITSELF BY FOUR MINUTES FOR WIFE'S SIXTIETH BIRTHDAY — "ENOUGH FOR ONE LAST DANCE TO A SONG THAT HAD ALREADY ENDED"

He had made clocks for forty years. He had learned, in that time, how to make time itself hesitate when something mattered enough to stay inside a moment a little longer.
By Aurelie Fontaine · Correspondence Desk · Virentis XIV

Elan Voss, seventy-one, has spent his adult life in the company of time. Forty years of clocks — pocket watches, parlour pieces, anniversary commissions from husbands who wanted one afternoon to go on a little longer than it had any business lasting. He understood time the way a carpenter understands wood: where it would hold, where it bent, and where, if you applied the right pressure in the right place, it would yield.

For Mira's sixtieth birthday, he adjusted the clocks of their town by four minutes. Not all the clocks — only the ones that governed their evening: the kitchen clock, the one in the square she passed on her walk home, the small enamel piece on her dressing table that had belonged to her grandmother. Four minutes. Enough for one more revolution around the sitting room to a song that had ended before either of them was ready to stop moving. He had found the recording from their wedding night. He had kept it all this time for exactly this kind of use.

"He played our song," Mira told this correspondent. "I didn't know where he'd found it — it had been so long. We danced, and then the song ended, and we kept dancing for a little while anyway, because the room was very warm and there was no reason to stop. I noticed the clock was wrong later. I didn't mention it. He didn't either. That was the gift."

The record of the adjustment exists only in Elan's private logbook, in an entry written the following morning beneath the date. It reads: M. did not want to stop. I gave her four more minutes. I would have given her four hundred years if I had worked out how. He has left the logbook on his workbench, open to that page, for the past three months. Mira has read it eleven times. She has not told him this.

Romance  ·  Literature

BOOKSHOP OWNER HID A LOVE LETTER ON PAGE 47 OF EVERY BOOK HE SOLD FOR THIRTY YEARS — FORTY-ONE LETTERS HAVE NOW COME BACK

He chose page forty-seven because that was how old she was when they first met, holding a Chekhov in a shop that smelled of rain.
By Celeste Beaumont · Books & Letters

Augustin Beaumont ran his secondhand bookshop in the Marais quarter for thirty years, and in every book he sold — every single one, through three decades and tens of thousands of volumes — he tucked a small folded note on page forty-seven, addressed to his wife Celeste. The notes were never the same. Some were passages he had underlined in other books. Some were memories: the specific sound of a Tuesday morning in autumn, the way she laughed at things that weren't quite funny but almost. Some were only the sentence: I am still glad it was raining that afternoon.

When early dementia began taking Augustin's memory last spring, Celeste sat down with the shop's thirty-year sales ledger and began writing to the addresses they had. Of the letters she sent, forty-one have come back — each containing the note from page forty-seven, kept by people who found it and could not throw it away. One man framed his and hung it above his wife's side of the bed. A woman in Lyon says she has carried hers in her wallet since 1997.

Celeste reads the returned notes to Augustin in the evenings. Some days he does not know they are his. Some days he listens and then laughs the way he used to, as if he has heard something very good for the first time.

Romance  ·  Botany

MAN SENDS ONE FLOWER EACH DAY FOR A YEAR — THE FINAL FLOWER IS AN EXTINCT SPECIES BORROWED FROM KEW GARDENS

By autumn she had learned a secret language. The last word: there are things that exist in almost no quantity. You are one of them.
By Flora Strand · Society Pages

Theo Marchetti sent Rosa one flower every morning for three hundred and sixty-five days. Each arrived before eight a.m., with a small card naming the flower and its meaning in the Victorian language of flowers — a system of communication once used by people who needed to say things that were not yet safe to say aloud. Red chrysanthemum: I love you. White heather: good luck. Violet: I think of you always. Fennel: I am worthy of your praise — which Rosa found audacious and wrote back saying so, using a fern: sincerity.

By September she was sending flowers back. He sent her a yellow tulip on a Thursday — there is sunshine in your smile — and she pressed it until it turned translucent as onion skin, which she told him was the most beautiful thing she'd done with a compliment.

On day three hundred and sixty-five, a white box arrived containing a Middlemist's Red — one of only three remaining specimens of its species on Earth, on temporary loan from Kew Gardens. The card read: There are things in this world that exist in almost no quantity at all. I borrowed this one for today. I would not borrow you for anything. I would only keep you, if you will. Rosa said yes before she had finished the sentence.

Romance  ·  Correspondence

PEN PALS DISCOVER AFTER SIX YEARS OF LETTERS THEY LIVE FOUR STREETS APART — RECOGNIZED EACH OTHER'S HANDWRITING BEFORE THEIR FACES

By Hilde Maas · Social Affairs · Essen

Maren and Sol joined Essen's postal correspondence club on the same afternoon in March, six years ago, without knowing it. They were paired by lot number and given only post-box addresses, as the club's tradition required. For six years they wrote about the particular and small: the quality of light on a Tuesday in November, the sound of their respective neighborhoods at six in the morning, a sentence from a book that had lodged in each of them for reasons they couldn't fully explain. They wrote about the bakery on their street. For three years, neither realized it was the same one.

At the club's annual gathering, Maren and Sol recognized each other's handwriting on their name tags before they recognized each other's faces. Sol stopped walking. Maren said she knew the moment she saw his name written in his own hand — she had spent six years reading that hand and could not have not known it in any light, in any room, under any circumstances.

"He looked at my name tag and went completely still," Maren said. "I understood why before he said anything. I had been reading his voice for six years. I already knew what it sounded like when he was surprised by something wonderful."
Romance  ·  Astronomy

ASTRONOMER SPENT 40 YEARS TRANSMITTING INTO EMPTY SPACE — THE RESPONSE ARRIVED ADDRESSED TO HER BY NAME

By Cosima Veil · Science & Heart

Dr. Vera Lindqvist sent her first transmission at twenty-two: Satie's Gymnopédie No. 1, her own voice reading a poem she had written that afternoon, and the precise coordinates of her grandmother's garden in Uppsala — where she had first understood, at seven years old, that the sky was not a ceiling but a floor pointing somewhere else entirely. She expected nothing back. She was a scientist. She transmitted anyway, once a year, for forty years. Her students called it love.

The signal received in 2019 lasted four minutes and twelve seconds. It contained: the Gymnopédie, slightly varied in the third movement, as if someone had been practicing it from memory for a very long time and had developed their own opinion about it. The coordinates of her grandmother's garden. And, encoded in radio-wave binary, her name — Vera — followed by one additional word.

Dr. Lindqvist has not disclosed what that word was. She has described it, in three separate interviews, only as "accurate." She has been playing the Gymnopédie more often since. When asked if she will transmit a response, she said: "I already did. Forty years ago. They just got it."

Science Fiction  ·  Continuum Studies

IN THE UNIVERSE WHERE MUSIC CAME BEFORE LANGUAGE, ALL LAWS ARE WRITTEN IN B-FLAT MINOR — THE GREAT FAMINE ACCORD IS DEVASTATING TO HEAR

Scholars confirm that in Stratum Seven, legislation is composed rather than drafted. The most moving piece of law ever written is also, by unanimous consensus, the most heartbreaking thing ever put to paper.
By Archive Correspondent · Thessandrian Institute of Parallel Studies · Virentis XIV

Scholars at the Thessandrian Archive of Continuum Studies have published their most complete survey to date of Stratum Seven — the universe in which tonal communication preceded verbal language by an estimated four thousand years. Early Stratum Seven humans developed sophisticated musical vocabularies long before they developed words. Grief was a descending minor third. Danger was a repeated flat seventh. Love, most researchers agree, was silence — a precise, attended silence that both parties understood as meaning exactly what it meant.

When verbal language finally emerged in Stratum Seven, it was structured around music rather than the reverse. All formal communication — legislation, contracts, judicial rulings, political speeches — is therefore composed in musical key. Minor keys signal matters of consequence; major keys, celebration and resolution; modal keys, ambiguity and philosophical uncertainty. Composers-in-law are among the most respected professionals in Stratum Seven society, trained for twelve years before being permitted to draft binding instruments.

"The Great Famine Accord of their year 1204 was set in B-flat minor," the Archive notes. "Hearing it performed in full is not simply moving. It is — and the panel used this word in their written summary with full awareness of its implications — honest. More honest than any document the panel had encountered in seven years of comparative legal study."

Their equivalent of a parliamentary debate is a forty-minute orchestral session. Amendments are submitted as harmonic variations. Dissent is registered by playing in a different key simultaneously, which is considered both a profound artistic statement and a tremendous nuisance to sit through. Two votes are currently before their upper chamber. One is in C major. The other, on the question of water rights, is in D minor, unresolved. It has been in session for nine years.

Science Fiction  ·  Technology

THE REVERSALIST EMPIRE: WHERE CAMERAS PHOTOGRAPH THE FUTURE — PORTRAITS ARE LEGALLY CLASSIFIED AS MARRIAGE PROPOSALS

By Temporal Affairs Bureau

Citizens of the Reversalist Empire — whose technology operates in reverse temporal sequence due to a manufacturing tradition involving left-handed assembly tools, which reverse the polarity of certain mechanisms — discovered in their year equivalent to 1902 that cameras so assembled display not what was, but what will be. A photograph taken in the Empire shows the subject as they will appear: minutes from now, or years, or — in the case of long-exposure portraits — decades hence, in a room you have not yet seen, with an expression belonging to a moment that has not yet arrived.

Photography in the Empire is considered among the most intimate of all acts. Wedding photographs, in which couples see themselves as they will appear at seventy on the day they marry at twenty-five, are universally described as "the most honest ceremony there is." Many couples report that seeing the photographs makes them less afraid. A few report that it changes who they choose.

"To take someone's photograph," the Empire's leading theorist wrote, "is to say: I have looked at your future and I am not frightened by what I found there. That is either a proposal or a profound act of friendship. It is never nothing."
Science Fiction  ·  Governance

MOON COLONY 7 ELECTS GOVERNOR WHO HAS NEVER SPOKEN — FIRST OFFICIAL ACT: A SINGLE SENTENCE ON THE COMMUNITY BOARD

By Luna Bureau Desk · Artemis-7

The eight hundred and forty-seven residents of Artemis-7 cast votes in their first independently administered election this cycle. The winner — Cass, thirty-one, systems technician — has not spoken aloud since childhood and ran no campaign in any conventional sense.

What Cass did, instead, for one year: sat with colonists during the oxygen shortage, when rationing required each resident to speak thirty percent less to preserve breathable air. Worked double shifts during the hull breach in Sector 4. Wrote one sentence per week on the colony's shared board: The light is different in the eastern corridor on Tuesdays. And: The children have learned to build things that hold. And: I think we are doing better than we know.

On the morning of the election result, a sixth sentence appeared on the board before anyone else was awake. It read: I will try. The colony's residents have, by informal survey, each read it an average of eleven times. No one has asked what it means. They appear, by all accounts, to already know.

Science Fiction  ·  Architecture

WHERE GRAVITY IS OPTIONAL ON TUESDAYS: THE AWARD-WINNING LIBRARY THAT SORTS ITS OWN BOOKS — FICTION ALWAYS DRIFTS TO THE WINDOWS

By Urban Planning Review · Floating Corridors

The Floating Corridor Cities, suspended above the Continental Shelf in universe HV-229, experience a six-hour gravity reduction every Tuesday due to a planetary alignment their physicists describe as "convenient and inexplicable, and we have decided to stop worrying about it." During the Tuesday window, residents, loose furniture, unsecured objects, and the contents of buildings may drift upward by as much as forty meters.

This year's civic architecture awards were dominated by libraries. The winner — in Ansen-Four — embedded magnetic genre-tags in the spine of every book in its collection. During Tuesday's uplift, books release from their shelves, sort by subject and emotional register, and return to the correct location without staff intervention. The system requires no power. It requires only the Tuesday.

The jury noted one anomaly: fiction, wherever it is replaced during the week, consistently drifts toward the windows during uplift. The architect described it, in his acceptance address, as "the library having an opinion," and said he had decided not to correct it, on the grounds that the library appears to know something he does not.

Science Fiction  ·  Alternate History

THE UNIVERSE WITHOUT WAR: THE MOON LANDING WAS A SOURDOUGH STARTER — BERNARD THE THOUSAND-YEAR-OLD DOUGH IS STILL ACTIVE AND BAKES ON THURSDAYS

By Theta-4 Anthropological Desk

Anthropological reports from Continuum Theta-4 confirm that without the development of organized conflict — warfare failed to take root in Theta-4, due to what researchers describe as a "profound early emphasis on grain and an extremely good first harvest in approximately 8,000 BCE that everyone agreed should be protected" — human civilizations redirected what would have been military expenditure into communal ovens, grain cultivation, and the architecture of pastry.

Their equivalent of the Eiffel Tower is a four-hundred-metre structure modeled on a croissant, laminated in copper that has turned the precise green of summer fields. Their equivalent of the Moon Landing, achieved in their year 1,204, was the successful cultivation of a sourdough starter kept continuously alive for one thousand years. The starter was named Bernard at year three hundred, by a democratic committee that considered forty-seven other names and rejected them on the grounds that they did not suit him.

"Bernard has survived two floods, one earthquake, a particularly bad summer in 1887, and a brief period in the fourteenth century during which three separate factions each claimed ownership of him," the archive's curator said. "He is resilient. We are very proud of him. He bakes on Thursdays. The bread is extraordinary."

PARLIAMENT OF CLOUDS ISSUES FORMAL RESOLUTION: HUMANS ARE "ADEQUATE" — THREE NIMBOSTRATUS ABSTAINED ON PRINCIPLE AND WERE LATER OBSERVED RAINING ON A WEDDING

The resolution passed 7,412 to 3. Humanity has been given a provisional approval rating, pending review next century. A subcommittee of altocumulus has been formed to monitor "the situation with the aerosols."
By Meteorological Affairs Desk · High Altitude Correspondent · Virentis XIV

After what climate historians estimate to be several centuries of informal deliberation — conducted in the form of weather systems, whose legislative implications no human institution had previously thought to decode — the International Parliament of Clouds convened above the Swiss plateau last Thursday to issue their first formal statement on the question of humanity. The chamber, a formation of cumulonimbus and stratocirrus some forty kilometres across and seventeen kilometres tall, was described by ground observers as "imposing," by one meteorologist as "frankly a bit much," and by a hiking club from Zurich, who were caught beneath it, as "extremely inconsiderate timing."

The resolution reads: Humanity is, on balance, adequate. They have interesting hats. Their fires are, on occasion, beautiful. Their music is sometimes acceptable and at other times deeply baffling. Their wars are exhausting and we are persistently asked to provide dramatic backdrop for them, which we find undignified and would prefer to stop. We are, however, on balance, willing to continue. The aerosols remain a concern. This will be reviewed.

"The three dissenting nimbostratus," the Institute noted, "have a documented history of strong opinions. They were also responsible for the unseasonal hailstorm at Wimbledon in 2019, the flooding during the outdoor production of Midsummer Night's Dream in Prague in 2021, and what one observer described as 'a very pointed drizzle' over the climate summit of the same year."

The Parliament has scheduled its next formal review for the following century, subject to atmospheric conditions. The full text of the resolution is not expected to be fully decoded for another twelve years. "They are clouds. They are not in a hurry," said the Institute's director. "Neither, it turns out, are we."

MAN WINS 14-YEAR LAWSUIT AGAINST HIS OWN SHADOW — SHADOW FILES IMMEDIATE COUNTERSUIT FOR "INCONSISTENT LIGHTING CHOICES AND AESTHETIC NEGLECT"

By Legal Affairs Desk · Marseille

Clement Ruiz, fifty-three, of Marseille, who filed suit in 2010 against his shadow for "following him without consent and reproducing his physical gestures without attribution or compensation," was awarded symbolic damages of one euro by the Sixth Marseille Civil Court on Tuesday. The ruling was delivered by Judge Ariane Petit, who is reported to have sighed three distinct times during the reading of the judgment and once more during the applause.

The shadow, represented by the boutique firm Ombre & Associés — which specialises in what its founding partner calls "non-corporeal civil claims, a field I am aware sounds made up and I accept that" — filed a countersuit within forty minutes of the verdict. The suit alleges emotional distress, specifically citing M. Ruiz's "documented and persistent tendency to walk into walls"; his "use of overhead fluorescent bathroom lighting, which the defendant describes as unflattering, continuous, and applied with apparent indifference"; and what the filing terms "a wilful and ongoing neglect of one's own silhouette as a site of aesthetic dignity."

Judge Petit has scheduled mediation for the following month. His shadow, it has been noted, will also be present, pending suitable lighting arrangements, which the court has agreed to provide and which have cost more than the awarded damages.

Humor  ·  Municipal Affairs

TOWN THAT ACCIDENTALLY BANNED HAPPINESS NOW LEADS ENTIRE REGION IN PRODUCTIVITY — TOURISM HAS TRIPLED, VISITORS DESCRIBE IT AS "A VERY ORGANISED EXHALE"

By Regional Affairs Desk · Central Bureau

Burgerheim, a municipality of six thousand in the central German interior, enacted a public conduct ordinance in 2019 intended to address complaints about noise from outdoor celebrations. The ordinance, as printed, criminalised "excessive emotional radiance in public spaces." The original draft had specified "excessive acoustic radiance." The translation error was not discovered for eleven months, by which point residents had read the ordinance, considered it, and largely complied on the grounds that it seemed, on reflection, reasonable.

The results have been, by nearly every measurable standard, excellent. Burgerheim now leads its administrative region in economic productivity, infrastructure maintenance, public transit punctuality, and library book return rates, which stand at ninety-seven point four percent. Tourism has tripled since 2021, with visitors describing the town as "the most profoundly reasonable place in Europe," "somewhere that feels like a very organised exhale," and, in one travel review, "the first place I have ever visited that seemed to know what it was doing."

The mayor, Rolf Steinmann, was asked by this correspondent whether he is happy with these results. He paused for eleven seconds, looked out the window at the efficiently maintained town square, and said: "I am satisfied." He has declined, for the fifth consecutive year, to correct the ordinance.

Humor  ·  Paranormal

LOCAL GHOST LEAVES ONE-STAR REVIEW FOR HOUSE HE'S HAUNTED SINCE 1887 — "CURRENT TENANTS ENTIRELY ON THEIR PHONES. AMBIANCE: RUINED."

By Digital & Spectral Affairs · Edinburgh

A property in Edinburgh's New Town district received a one-star review on the hospitality platform Yelp this Thursday, from an account registered under the name "Frederick H., Resident Since 1887." The account has no profile photograph. It has no other reviews. It joined the platform, records indicate, at 3:17 a.m. on Thursday morning. The platform's verification team has confirmed the account is real, and has declined to elaborate on what "real" means in this context.

The review reads, in full: Have been haunting this property for one hundred and thirty-seven years. In that time I have appeared in mirrors. I have moved objects from tables to floors. I have produced cold spots on fourteen occasions and unexplained sounds in the attic on eleven more. Previous tenants (1952–1978) were excellent: screamed appropriately, called a vicar, left within the year. Current tenants: entirely on their telephones. I appeared in the bathroom mirror last Tuesday for a full four minutes. Not one of them looked up. Ambiance of the property has been completely ruined by the ring lights. Would not haunt again. One star. I am, I should note, contractually obligated to remain. I have reviewed the terms extensively and there is no exit clause.

The current tenants said they had always found the cold spots "quite a charming feature" and had been meaning to look into underfloor heating.

Humor  ·  Breaking Domestic

DEATH ARRIVES AT WRONG ADDRESS, STAYS FOR TEA, ADMIRES THE CURTAINS, DEPARTS WITH A VERY THOUGHTFUL HANDWRITTEN CONDOLENCE CARD

By Correspondent at Large

Mrs. Eugenia Voss, eighty-one, of number fourteen, answered her front door at eleven-fifteen on Tuesday morning to find Death, who consulted his ledger and immediately admitted, with evident and genuine embarrassment, that he had the wrong address. He had been looking for a Mrs. Eloise Voss of number forty, two streets over. He had misread his own handwriting. "He showed me the ledger," Mrs. Voss told this correspondent. "He was right — it is quite difficult to read. I told him so and he said he was aware of the problem and had been meaning to do something about it for some time."

Death came inside, which Mrs. Voss described as "polite of him, under the circumstances." He sat at the kitchen table. He accepted two biscuits. He admired the curtains in the sitting room — hand-embroidered, forty years old — which she found "unexpectedly perceptive." They discussed the garden for approximately fifteen minutes. He said it was very well maintained for the season. She said she had had help. He said he knew, and left a small pause that Mrs. Voss reports she found "not unpleasant."

Death left a card before departing. It read: My sincere apologies for the confusion. You will know when it is time. It will not be for a while yet. In the meantime, the garden is genuinely excellent, and those really are very good curtains. Yours, etc. Mrs. Voss has put the card on the mantelpiece. She says it is the most useful piece of correspondence she has received in years.
